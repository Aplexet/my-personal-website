From a Trading Idea to a Backtest: My Experience with Agentic Trading Lab

Published July 29, 2026

Starting a personal website felt like the perfect small project: it gave me room to combine structure, design, and my own personality. I decided to push my technical limits further by testing whether an AI trading agent could successfully execute investment ideas inside the Agentic Trading Lab (ATL) software platform using mock paper money instead of real capital.

Why I Started

I wanted a place where I could introduce myself and keep track of what I create. Building a website also helped me see how a simple idea becomes something real through many small decisions. Similarly, exploring ATL allowed me to take abstract quantitative theories and spin up virtual systems to systematically track return and risk over past market timelines.

At first, an empty page felt intimidating. Once I divided it into a header, sections, cards, and a footer, the project became much easier to understand. The same mental logic helped me find my way around the ATL dashboard interface, as I stepped smoothly through the global portfolio indicators on the Home screen, configured setups on the My Agents canvas, evaluated ranking metrics on the Competition page, and browsed ideas built by peers inside the Community marketplace.

Engineering Meets Art

HTML gave the page its structure, almost like a frame or blueprint. CSS let me work with color, spacing, shape, and balance. I liked seeing technical rules create a visual result. This process directly mirrored the structured five-step scientific framework required by the lab to ensure a completely fair backtesting comparison between models.

To keep variables completely isolated, I deployed a fixed initial capital sum of $1,000, limited the tradable pool exclusively to Dow Jones 30 stocks, and isolated a shared historical date window. My core hypothesis stated that an aggressive Momentum Scout agent would yield higher rewards but suffer a larger maximum drawdown than a defensive Balanced Starter configuration.

The red, black, and white palette keeps the design focused, while geometric details connect the page to both engineering and art. When configuring the user instructions, I had the agent follow clear logic: "Spread the money across a few strong stocks. Buy on meaningful dips, take profits after strong increases, and do not put all the money into one stock."

Clicking "Run Backtest" sent the status immediately into compiling mode, running through data bars in less than 45 seconds. The resulting metrics perfectly verified my hypothesis: the Momentum Scout scored the highest return but produced a rocky path with a steep drawdown, while the Balanced Starter maintained a very smooth equity curve.

What Worked Well

During my evaluation of the platform, I discovered several great core strengths:

  • Low barrier to entry: You can quickly construct and spin up complex natural language algorithms without handling data pipelines.
  • Clear developer workflow: The software naturally guides users through a loop of Configure → Backtest → Review → Improve.
  • Built-in reference baselines: Having Buy-and-Hold and the DJIA market metrics loaded automatically gives an objective reality check on whether an agent is genuinely skilled.

What Could Be Improved

Despite the smooth interface, a few things were confusing or difficult to manage:

  • I experienced card sorting confusion. This made distinguishing multiple deployed active agents difficult because names alone look uniform. ATL could improve this by integrating unique model labels or customizable strategy tags.
  • I found performance analysis tedious because checking distinct equity curves required clicking between separate pages. ATL could improve this by adding a single side-by-side comparison screen.
  • I encountered an initial problem where expensive stocks were never bought, which confused me until I discovered the hidden single-position asset equity limit of ~$250. ATL should display these system rule constraints on the interface before running a simulation.

What I Learned

Here are a few lessons I am taking into my next project:

  • Large projects feel simpler when they are divided into sections.
  • Clear names and comments make code easier to update.
  • Good design should work on both computers and phones.
  • It is okay to improve an idea one version at a time.
  • A historical backtest is a safe simulation, not an absolute guarantee of future real-world profitability.
  • Financial metrics mean nothing in a vacuum; return data must always be carefully cross-examined against risk drawdowns and Sharpe ratios.

Conclusion

This website is only a beginning. I am excited to keep learning, add future projects, and use this blog to document what I discover. Working through Agentic Trading Lab showed me that combining structured computer systems with raw personal creativity can transform abstract concepts into measurable, educational applications.
